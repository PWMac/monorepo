1What it is

Oracle Edge reads every U.S.-listed company's annual reports straight from the SEC, keeps only the businesses that pass Oracle-style tests, works out what each survivor is worth, and ranks the ones selling at a sensible price. After every market close it refreshes itself, logs a buy signal when a stock enters the Buy Zone and a sell signal when the business stops qualifying, and tracks how those signals would have performed.

One click, all local
Runs on your PC in your browser. One folder holds the code, the Python runtime and the SQLite database. Nothing is uploaded anywhere.
Explainable
Every score, price and signal shows the numbers and the rule behind it, in plain English: "Return on equity 14.9% (limit 15.0%)".
Testable
The same engine replays history from 2019 using only the data available on each day, so you can see whether a rule change actually helps.
What it isn't. It isn't financial advice, a trading bot or a real-time quote screen. It works from end-of-day prices and published filings, and it suggests actions for the next market open. You place any trades yourself.

2Quick start

  1. Install: double-click Install-OracleEdge.bat in the oracle-edge folder of your code (for example C:\repos\monorepo\oracle-edge). It copies the code exactly as it is in that folder, local edits included, to C:\OracleEdge (keeping any existing data), puts a desktop shortcut in place and starts the app. Nothing is downloaded from GitHub. To upgrade after changing the code, run it again: it first stops the copy that's running in the background (so the new version is the one you see), keeps your data, and the database upgrades itself. The version you're running is shown at the bottom of the app's left menu.
  2. Start: double-click Oracle Edge on the desktop (it runs C:\OracleEdge\Start.bat). The first start downloads its private Python (about a minute). Your browser opens at http://127.0.0.1:8765.
  3. Setup wizard: enter a name and email for the SEC (they require it for downloads), choose the history start (default 2019) and start the first-time load. It downloads all company filings once (about 1.3 GB), scores every company, fetches prices for the shortlist and replays history. It's a long one-time job; you can close the browser and come back.
  4. Settings: set up email (optional), the daily run time and the backup folder, then click Save & (re)register task so Windows runs the update after each close, even when the app isn't open.
  5. Set your money: in Profiles → Portfolio, enter your starting money and what you add each week.
  6. Pick holdings and a new-money rule: open Backtest → Find the best number of holdings, run it from 2019, and save the winner to a profile (section 13). Then Rebuild live signals so daily signals use it.
  7. Use it: open Today each evening or read the email. Click any ticker for its full report card.

Press / anywhere to search for a ticker or company name.

By default the daily run waits until 6:00 PM New York time, which leaves time for closing prices to settle. It skips weekends and market holidays. If your PC was off, the app catches up the next time it starts.

If FRED stops answering, the app gives up after three failed series in a row, keeps the data it already has, and tries again at the next daily update. The VIX then comes from Yahoo (^VIX), and so do exchange rates FRED doesn't publish (for example the Chilean and Peruvian peso).
Why filing dates matter. A company's 2025 results aren't known until its 10-K is filed, often in February or March. Every calculation uses only facts filed on or before the day being evaluated. That's what keeps the backtests honest: no peeking at numbers nobody had yet ("look-ahead bias").
Why free first. SEC data is the original source that paid vendors repackage, so the fundamentals lose nothing. Prices are where free sources wobble, so every source is a plug-in with automatic fallback, and paying later is a settings change, not a rewrite.

5Which companies are screened

  • Size and tradability: market cap of at least $100 million and at least $250,000 traded per day. The floors are deliberately low because the Oracle has said that with small sums he'd hunt among small, overlooked companies. The volume floor only keeps a stock practical to buy.
  • Foreign companies that file with the SEC (20-F / 40-F, many large ADRs) are included and valued in U.S. dollars per traded share. Companies listed only abroad aren't in the free data.
  • Routing by industry: banks (SIC 6021–6036) and insurers (6311–6411) get their own rulebooks. REITs, SPACs, blank-check shells and closed-end funds are excluded because owner-earnings math doesn't describe them. Everything else uses the standard rules.
  • Enough history: at least 7 annual reports with the core line items. Ten are used when available.

6Scoring the business (Quality, 0–100)

Quality answers one question: is this a wonderful business? The answer doesn't depend on the share price.

Gates: must pass

A few rules are pass/fail. A company that fails any gate can't be bought, however cheap it is. For standard companies the gates are:

  • profitable in 7 of 10 years at minimum (10 of 10 scores full marks);
  • long-term debt no more than about 4 years of earnings (8 is the floor);
  • 10-year average return on equity of 15% or more, with no single year under 10%;
  • enough data.

Two more gates apply only to buying: margin of safety and expected return (see section 8).

Scored rules: three anchor points

Every rule has a floor (scores 0), a target (scores 70) and an excellent level (scores 100), with straight lines in between. "Target" is the Oracle's rule of thumb, so a company that just meets every rule of thumb scores about 70.

Each rule's score blends how good the 10-year figure is with how consistently the company hit the target year by year:

rule score = 0.6 × level score + 0.4 × (share of years at or better than target × 100)

Rules are averaged within five categories, then the categories are combined:

CategoryWeightAsks
Profitability35%Fat, stable margins and steadily growing earnings: signs of a moat
Returns25%High returns on equity and on invested capital
Balance sheet20%Little debt, plenty of cash, growing retained earnings
Cash10%Profits that turn into real cash without heavy spending on equipment
Shareholder10%Buybacks rather than dilution, and each retained dollar creating at least a dollar of market value

Quality ≥ 70 is needed for the Buy Zone; 80 or more can make a buy a STRONG BUY.

Confidence (0.85–1.00) trims the rank slightly when recent line items are missing, history is short, or reported profits don't convert well into cash. It never boosts a score.

Why level and consistency. The Oracle looks for durable advantages. A company that averaged 20% ROE thanks to two spectacular years is very different from one that earned 18% every year. The consistency term rewards the second.

Why separate rulebooks. A bank has no gross margin, and its "debt" (customer deposits) is its raw material. 8Judging the price (Value, 0–100)

A wonderful business is only a good investment at the right price. Two deliberately conservative models estimate intrinsic value per share, and the app uses the lower value and the lower expected return.

Model A: owner-earnings DCF
Starts from owner earnings (net income + depreciation − capex) per share. Grows them at the lowest of the sustainable growth rate, the 10-year EPS growth rate and 15%, fading to 3% by year 10. Discounts at the 30-year Treasury rate, plus 2 points while that rate is under 5%. The terminal value is capped at 20× earnings.
Model B: 10-year projection (Buffettology)
Projects EPS 10 years out at the sustainable growth rate (ROE × retained share, capped at 20%). Multiplies by the company's own median P/E (capped at 20) and adds dividends. That gives the expected annual return from today's price, and the price that would earn exactly 10%.

If only one model can run (for example, owner earnings are negative), its value is cut by a further 15%.

OutputHow
Margin of safety1 − price ÷ intrinsic value. At least 25% is needed to buy.
Expected 10-yr returnLower of the two models. At least 10% a year is needed to buy (the "Buffettology 15%" profile asks for 15%).
Buy belowLower of value × 0.75 and the price that gives a 10% return
Strong buy belowValue × 0.60 (an extra 15% margin)
Value score (0–100)Scores margin of safety, expected return and earnings yield against bonds on the same floor / target / excellent scale

Banks and insurers are valued on book value instead. The price-to-book multiple that their return on equity justifies is (ROE − growth) ÷ (required return − growth), capped at 3×. Banks use tangible book. The lower of that and the 10-year projection is used. Foreign filers are converted to dollars with Federal Reserve exchange rates and divided by the ADR ratio so the value matches the U.S.-traded share. If the implied P/E is implausible (under 3 or over 150), the stock stays pending rather than showing a wrong number.

Why the Treasury rate. The Oracle has said he discounts at the long-term government rate and gets his safety from a large margin of safety rather than a high discount rate. The 2-point add-on keeps very low rates from inflating values. The 10% bar reflects his long-held expectation for a good equity investment.
Why two models and the lower one. Each model fails in different ways: a DCF is sensitive to the discount rate, a P/E projection to the exit multiple. Taking the lower answer is margin of safety built into the method.

Comparing stocks side by side

Click the In the Buy Zone, Wonderful, but pricey or Model holdings card on Today to open that list as a sortable table (the other two cards open Market and Performance). Each list shows the same columns, so stocks can be compared the way the Oracle would:

  • The business first. Sort by Quality or ROE. Look for 15%+ returns on equity with little debt (debt payback under 3–4 years), steady earnings (Predictability) and real cash (cash conversion near 100%). High, stable gross margin and ROIC with low capex hint at a moat.
  • Management. A shrinking share count (buybacks) and a $1 test above 1 mean retained earnings are used well.
  • Then the price. Sort by margin of safety or expected 10-year return. On the pricey list, "% above buy price" shows which great businesses are closest to buyable.

The business columns are 10-year figures from the latest annual report. Banks and insurers use their own rulebooks, so some of those columns are blank for them. Hover any column header for a one-line explanation.

11When it sells, and why so rarely

The Oracle's favorite holding period is "forever". He sells for a few reasons: the business has deteriorated, he made a mistake, the price has become absurd, or he needs the money for something clearly better. Oracle Edge follows the same rules and never sells because the price fell. A lower price for an intact business is an ADD, not a SELL.

REVIEW: an early warning before a SELL

TriggersWhat happens
HardQuality down 10+ points since purchase · trailing-12-month loss · debt payback over its limit · ROE (ROA for banks) below its floor, on 10-Q numbersREVIEW now. Becomes a SELL if the next filing confirms it. New buys are blocked while it's open.
SoftProfits far above operating cash flow (cash under 50% of profit) · share count up 5%+ in a year · a gate within 10% of its limitREVIEW as a watch item only. Never causes a sale.
Soft: deteriorationReturn on new capital (incremental ROIC, 3-year windows) down three years running by 10+ points · gross margin below the year-earlier quarter three quarters in a row · acquisitions over 3 years at least 1.5× the revenue added (growth bought, not built) · receivables or inventory growing 15+ points faster than sales · stock-based pay 25%+ of free cash flow and rising · a disclosed concentration (often one customer) of 20%+REVIEW as a watch item; no ADDs while it's open. Never causes a sale.

The deterioration checks catch companies that still look fine on 10-year averages but are starting to slip. Customer concentration only shows when a company reports it as a plain figure; most report it only in the notes, which the SEC data doesn't carry. The checks can be switched off per profile ("Extra early warnings").

Quarterly early warnings use 10-Q filings to build trailing-12-month figures (this year's year-to-date + last full year − last year's year-to-date), so problems show up months before the next annual report.

Why wait for confirmation. One bad quarter is noise surprisingly often. Requiring the next filing to confirm a hard warning avoids selling a great business on a blip, while still acting within a quarter when the problem is real. The Oracle has written that his costliest errors include being slow to correct mistakes, so the confirmation is one filing, not "wait and hope".

Trimming

  • Nosebleed: price at 2× value and P/E at twice its 10-year median, for 10 straight days. Sell a third.
  • Oversized: a holding above 40% of the portfolio (or 1.5 ÷ number of holdings when you own few) is trimmed back to 30% (or an equal share). It only applies with 2 or more holdings.

12Price targets and alerts

The house rule: value decides what price is good; the chart only helps with when. Every buy, strong-buy and trim price comes from the valuation. Moving averages and Fibonacci levels never create a signal on their own. They only choose a smarter limit price inside the Buy Zone.

What the stock page shows

LevelHow it's set
Suggested buy limitIf the price is above the Buy Zone: the highest support level at or below the buy-below price (or the buy-below price itself). If already in the zone: a support within one average day's move below today's price, otherwise buy at the open.
Support levels50- and 200-day moving averages, Fibonacci retracements (38.2%, 50%, 61.8%) of the last 52 weeks' move, and the 52-week low
Buying in thirdsWhile the price is below a falling 50-day average (a "falling knife"): ⅓ at the limit, ⅓ at the next support at least one daily range lower, ⅓ at the strong-buy price
Trim above2× intrinsic value (the nosebleed level)
TrendUptrend (above a rising 200-day average), downtrend (below a falling 50-day average) or sideways

The price chart overlays the 50- and 200-day averages, the Fibonacci levels (purple, over the last 52 weeks) and the suggested limit on top of the value and buy-below lines.

Alerts

  • Watched: Buy Zone, pricey and near-miss stocks (limit, buy-below, strong-buy prices); stocks you own (strong-buy add zone and trim level); and your own alerts, any price "falls to" or "rises to", added on a stock's page.
  • Approaching = within 1.5× the stock's average daily range (ATR-14), never closer than 2% or farther than 8%.
  • Reached = the close is through the target, or the day's low or high touched it (when a resting limit order would have filled).
  • No repeats: each target alerts once when approaching and once when reached. It re-arms after the price moves back by twice the band.
  • Alerts appear on Today and in the email digest. The very first check after setup or an upgrade only records where each stock stands, so you aren't flooded.
Why not trade the chart? The research is weak. Large long-run studies find moving-average timing rules show no reliable edge in recent decades and give many false signals. Fibonacci studies are few and small. The best-supported idea is that value and momentum work better together, which justifies one gentle tool: don't buy a cheap stock all at once while it's still sliding. Support levels are still useful as places to rest an order, because so many traders watch them.
Why a volatility band? A fixed "within 3%" is a week away for a utility and an hour away for a volatile stock. Measuring in average daily ranges warns about one to two typical days ahead for both.

To test the timing idea on real history, the locked profile Oracle Classic + timing uses the same rules but holds new buys while the price is below a falling 50-day average (for up to 60 signal days). Backtest it against Oracle Classic before switching it on.

13Model portfolio: how many stocks, and where new money goes

Several badges depend on whether you own a stock (HOLD vs WATCH, ADD, SELL, TRIM), so the app keeps a model portfolio that follows every signal. How it's built is set per profile under Profiles → Portfolio, so backtests can test it.

Focused (the default): your money, the best N stocks

  • Real dollars. The portfolio starts with your starting money (default $10,000) and adds your weekly amount (default $250) every week. Set both to what you actually invest.
  • At most N stocks. Each profile holds at most N stocks (try 5–12). A new stock's first purchase is about 1/N of the portfolio (1.5× for a STRONG BUY).
  • Weights aren't equal, and don't need to be. Each holding grows with its price and with the new money it gets, so weights drift apart naturally. The only limits: new money never pushes one stock above 25% of the portfolio, and a holding that grows past 40% is trimmed back to 30%.
  • Upgrades, not churn. When all slots are full, a new idea must rank much higher than the weakest holding to replace it (SWAP), and only holdings owned for a year or more can be swapped.

New money each week: which stock gets it?

Every week the cash (new money, plus anything from sales or trims) goes through one of five rules. Under every rule it only buys stocks that are in the Buy Zone with the business intact (no open REVIEW, Quality not slipping), and it never buys an expensive stock just to stay invested. If nothing qualifies, the cash waits, the way Berkshire's does.

RuleWhere the money goesWhen it makes sense
Best value (default)The stock, owned or new, with the highest expected 10-year return, i.e. the cheapest against its value. When that one reaches its cap, the next best.The Oracle's "buy the most value per dollar". Money is invested right away, but only at a margin of safety.
Best RankThe highest Oracle Rank first (quality weighted more than price).When you'd rather add to the best business than the cheapest.
Spread evenlySplit equally across every qualifying stock.The smoothest; closest to classic dollar-cost averaging.
PatientHolds cash until a stock reaches a strong-buy price (an extra 15% margin of safety). After 26 weeks of waiting it falls back to best value."Stockpile for the fat pitch". The holdings test shows whether waiting paid off.
Sliding reserveBuys part now and saves the rest. How much of the week's money is spent slides with how good the price is: at an ordinary Buy Zone price, 50%; at a strong-buy price, 100%; in between, a sliding share. The unspent part builds a reserve (at most 20% of the portfolio), and the reserve itself is only spent on a strong buy, where better prices get a bigger share. If no strong buy comes within 26 weeks, the reserve goes into the best of what's available. All three numbers are on the profile.A middle road between investing right away and waiting: most money works now, and there's dry powder when something gets really cheap.
Chosen setup: Buffettology 15%, at most 7 holdings, new money by the sliding reserve, strong-buy funding off. The tests behind that choice are in Why "Buffettology 15% · best 7".

When every slot is full and a strong buy appears

Turn on "trim or sell clearly weaker holdings to buy a strong buy" in the profile (it works with any rule). When a stock you don't own reaches a strong-buy price and all N slots are taken, the app funds it from holdings whose expected 10-year return is at least 4 points lower (the same gap swaps use). Holdings that aren't clearly worse are never touched.

  • First, an extra slot: the strong buy can take one slot above N. It uses the reserve and spare cash first, then trims slices of a few weaker holdings (at most a third of any one), starting with the most expensive, until it has a full position.
  • If the extra slot is already used, it sells the single weakest holding outright, but only one held at least 180 days.

Every trim and sale says which strong buy it pays for, for example "trim 25% to fund strong buy XYZ: its exp. return 19% vs 11% here".

Taxes: the model ignores them. In a taxable account, trimming or selling a winner held less than a year is taxed at higher short-term rates, so check before acting on a funding trim there. In an IRA it doesn't matter.

Each week's decision appears as a signal with a dollar amount, e.g. "ADD XYZ: put $250 into it: best value you own (exp. 10-yr return 16.2%, MOS 31%)", on Today and in the email. That answers "where should this week's money go?".

Why invest now rather than wait for a dip? Schwab's long-running study of $2,000 a year over many 20-year periods found the cost of waiting for the perfect moment usually exceeds the benefit of even perfect timing. Investing immediately came a close second to an imaginary perfect timer, and staying in cash came last. Vanguard found that investing a lump sum beat spreading it out about two-thirds (68%) of the time, because markets rise more often than they fall. So the defaults invest new money as soon as there's a good price. The app adds the Oracle twist: a good price is required, so cash waits when nothing in the Buy Zone qualifies. Use the holdings test to see whether "Patient" did better on your history.

How many, and which rule? Let your own history decide

Backtest → Find the best number of holdings replays the profile once and runs a portfolio for every combination of 5, 6, 8, 10 and 12 stocks × the new-money rules (the five above, plus spread evenly and sliding reserve each with "sell weaker holdings to fund strong buys" turned on), each starting with your starting money and adding your weekly amount, plus the old variable style for reference. Companies are scored once, so the whole test takes about as long as one backtest. For each it shows the yearly return, the result in dollars, what the same money put into the S&P 500 on the same days would be worth, and the downside (worst drop and worst 12 months) next to the S&P 500's. The recommended combination is the one that beat the S&P 500 most reliably:

  • The period is split into thirds and each combination is judged on the middle of its three results, so one lucky stretch can't win.
  • Each size is averaged with its neighbors under the same rule, so a single spiky result can't win.
  • A penalty applies for drawdowns deeper than the S&P 500's own worst drop.
  • On a tie, fewer holdings wins.

Save to profile stores the holdings and the new-money rule. A locked profile gets a copy, for example "Buffettology 20% · best 8". Then use Recalculate on a backtest, or Rebuild live signals, to apply it.

Why concentrate? The Oracle wrote in his 1993 letter that an investor who understands business economics and can find five to ten sensibly priced companies with durable advantages doesn't need conventional diversification. Research on fund managers' "best ideas" (Cohen, Polk and Silli) finds that their highest-conviction picks beat the market and the rest of their own portfolios. On your own data (2019–2026), 5–12 holdings beat 20–30 under both Buffettology and Classic.
Neighboring sizes can differ a lot (in one test 12 stocks earned 20% a year and 20 stocks 7%). That mostly reflects which particular stocks happened to fill the slots, so treat the recommendation as a range rather than an exact number. Fewer holdings also means bumpier results.

Why "Buffettology 15% · best 7" was chosen

Over the same dates the S&P 500 returned about 16% a year, the same money in it ended near $215,000–$219,000 (price data was refreshed between tests), its worst drop was −34% and its worst 12 months −20%.

The model also ignores taxes, which would make it worse.

What the test can't tell you

  • One period. 2019–2026 is about 7 years with one sharp crash (2020) and one bear market (2022). Gaps of 1–3 points a year between setups are within luck.
  • Survivorship bias. Free price sources leave out delisted stocks, so results are probably optimistic. A paid source removes this.
  • No taxes, fees or slippage. Real returns will be somewhat lower.
  • Re-check yearly. Run the holdings test again each year (or after a big market move) and keep the setup if it still holds up.

14Market temperature (the Buffett Indicator and the Bitcoin early warning)

Two readings are tracked every day, on Today and the Market page. Neither is a hard stop: the model keeps following its rules. When either one reaches an extreme, Today shows a Market temperature card with a recommendation, and the daily email includes a market alert (sent once, when a reading moves into or out of an extreme).

The Buffett Indicator

Total U.S. stock market value divided by GDP, which the Oracle once called "probably the best single measure of where valuations stand." It's built from the Fed's quarterly Z.1 market value of nonfinancial corporate equities and GDP, then carried forward daily using the total-market ETF (VTI). This matches the published readings (about 220–230% in 2026). Versions before 0.11.1 used the all-sectors series, which also counts financial companies and read about 1.7 times higher.

The Bitcoin early warning

Bitcoin's change over the last 20 trading days (Settings → Market temperature changes the window), compared with its own history. In past data its extremes went with the next month in tech stocks (QQQ): after Bitcoin fell into its worst 20% so far, QQQ rose over the following month much less often than usual; after a rise into its best 20%, more often. The middle of the range told nothing. It works as a warning about the coming weeks, not as a forecast of how far QQQ will move.

ReadingWhenSuggestion
Bearish extreme (warning)Bitcoin's 20-day change is in its worst 20% so farHold off adding new money to tech-heavy holdings for the coming weeks; if you want less risk, trim part of a position rather than selling all of it.
NormalAnywhere in betweenNothing to do.
Bullish extreme (tailwind)In its best 20% so farHistorically a supportive month. Nothing to do.
  • No peeking. Each day's cut-offs come only from the days before it, so the history on the Market page shows what the rule would have said at the time. Bitcoin is read at each stock-market close, so "20 days" means 20 trading days.
  • Evidence. The Market page shows what QQQ did after each kind of reading, with the number of separate episodes. Days within an episode overlap, so a handful of episodes is thin evidence, however many days they span.
  • Live record. Every daily update logs that day's call. Once 20 trading days have passed, QQQ's result is filled in, so the rule builds a record on data it has never seen. Check it on the Market page before leaning on it.
  • Was it right? The Market page scores every past call. A warning (▼) was right if QQQ fell over the next 20 trading days; a tailwind (▲) was right if QQQ rose. On the chart each call is a triangle at the day it began: green when it was right, red when it was wrong, grey while the 20 days are still running; the shaded bands show how long each reading lasted (red for warnings, green for tailwinds), with QQQ underneath so you can see what followed. Above the chart, two scores compare each kind of call with the base rate, how often QQQ fell (or rose) in any 20-day window. A call adds information only when it beats that base rate; a warning that is "right" 48% of the time when QQQ falls in 45% of all windows is barely better than a coin with QQQ's usual lean. The table under the chart lists every call, newest first.
  • Both at once. An expensive market and a Bitcoin selloff together is the most cautious setting: keep new purchases small until at least one eases.
  • Data. Bitcoin (BTC-USD) and QQQ come from the price sources (Stooq, then Yahoo) with every daily update.

  • Backtest replays any date range (default 2019 → today) day by day or week by week with any profile. It uses only the filings and prices known on each date, fills at the next open, and reports return vs the S&P 500, drawdowns, episodes and how each rank bucket performed afterwards.
  • Recalculate (on each backtest, or "Recalculate all") re-runs it over the same dates with the profile's current rules and portfolio settings and replaces the old results. Use it after changing the number of holdings.

  • Profiles hold every rule, threshold and setting. Shipped profiles are locked:

19Why it works this way

Why rules of thumb instead of machine learning?
Every decision can be traced to a number and a rule you can read and change. A model that "likes" a stock for unexplainable reasons would be the opposite of the Oracle's "invest in what you understand".
Why 10 years of history?
A full business cycle shows whether margins and returns survive a recession. The Oracle looks for long, consistent records. The 5-year profile exists only to let backtests start earlier.
Why wait two closes before buying?
A single day's price drop can push a stock into the zone and right back out. Two closes in a row filters that noise at little cost for a long-term holder.
Why act at the next open, not the close?
Signals are computed after the close, so the earliest realistic trade is the next open. Backtests fill there too, so the results are achievable.
Why a model portfolio if I have my own account?
It gives every stock a complete answer from day one, measures whether the signals beat the market, and makes size-based rules (trim, swap) work. Your real holdings can sit alongside it.
Why only the best N stocks?
The old style bought everything that qualified and averaged 64 holdings, which made it a broad value fund that lagged the S&P 500. The Oracle concentrates on his best ideas, and fund research finds the top-conviction picks do the work. A fixed number of slots forces each new idea to beat what you already own.
Why must a holding be owned a year before it can be swapped?
Without it the portfolio rotates out of winners as soon as they get pricier and something cheaper appears: lots of trading, taxes and missed compounding. The Oracle's "sell something cheap to buy something cheaper" is for clearly better ideas, not small rank differences.
Why is new money only added to stocks still in the Buy Zone?
"Keep investing in the best" means adding where the price still offers a margin of safety. Money isn't pushed into expensive stocks just to stay fully invested; it waits, as Berkshire's cash does.
Why not hold all new money for a crash?
Large studies (Schwab, Vanguard) find that waiting for a better moment usually costs more than it saves, because markets rise more often than they fall. The "Patient" rule lets you test that on your own picks instead of taking it on faith.
Why local and SQLite?
No server to run, no subscription, no data leaving your PC. One file is easy to back up, copy and inspect. SQLite handles this volume easily.
Why all Python with NiceGUI?
One language for data, rules and screens makes it easier to maintain and share. NiceGUI provides polished tables and charts without a separate web front end.

20Known limits

  • End of day only. Prices and alerts use daily closes; intraday alerts need a real-time feed (planned with Schwab).
  • Free price data can have gaps. The Data page lists missing tickers; adding a Tiingo or EODHD key fixes most.
  • Company-tagging quirks. Companies tag XBRL differently. The concept map covers common variations, and a few line items are built from their parts when a company doesn't report the total: SG&A from selling/marketing plus general and administrative; D&A from depreciation plus amortization; operating income from revenue minus total costs and expenses, or pre-tax income plus interest when there is no operating-income line; SG&A from gross profit minus operating income minus R&D when a company tags SG&A only with its own custom tag (this errs on the high side). Banks that stopped tagging total loans use their latest tagged year for loans-to-deposits. Some gaps are real and stay empty: railroads, card networks, airlines and asset managers report no cost of sales, so they have no gross margin (and no margins measured against gross profit). Companies with several share classes (Hershey, Visa) don't tag a company-wide EPS, so their EPS growth stays blank. Life insurers don't report reserve development. Missing checks are left out of the score rather than counted as failures, and confidence drops a little. Data → Coverage gaps lists only the gaps that better tagging could fix.
  • Insurer combined ratio is approximated from pre-tax and investment income when a company doesn't tag claims and expenses separately; the report card says "(approximated)".
  • Auditor changes aren't checked: the free data doesn't reliably include auditor names.
  • Foreign filers file annually, so they get no quarterly early warnings.
  • 10-Qs are pulled only for shortlisted and owned companies, to keep the daily run fast.
  • Backtests may suffer some survivorship bias (see section 15).
  • Cash earns nothing in the model portfolio, so periods with idle cash (more likely with the Patient rule or a strict profile like Buffettology 20%) slightly understate returns compared with real T-bills.
  • Fractional shares are assumed, so small weekly amounts buy exactly the dollar amount shown. At your broker, round to whole shares or use fractional-share buying where it's offered.
  • The holdings test has about eight years of history, which includes one major crash (2020) and a growth-led market. A different decade could favor a different number, so treat its answer as a range.
  • Judgment still matters. Moat durability, management integrity and whether you understand the business are your call; use the checklist on each report card.
