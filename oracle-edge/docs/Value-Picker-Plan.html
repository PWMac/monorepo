A one-click, portable, local stock screener that thinks like the Oracle. It keeps only the companies that pass his tests, ranks the ones selling at a sensible price, and logs a buy or sell signal whenever a stock enters or leaves that list. It refreshes itself after every market close.

How it works, end to end:

  1. Gather. After each market close it pulls end-of-day prices and checks the SEC for any new 10-K or 10-Q filings.
  2. Measure. For each company it calculates about 30 Oracle metrics over a 10-year window, covering both the level of each number and how consistent it has been.
  3. Sieve. Hard Gate rules drop anything that fails outright. The rest get a 0–100 Quality score.
  4. Price it. Two conservative intrinsic-value models give each company an expected 10-year return and a margin of safety, which together make the Value score.
  5. Rank. Oracle Rank = Quality0.65 × Value0.35 × Confidence. Both have to be good, and business quality counts a bit more than cheapness: "a wonderful company at a fair price" beats "a fair company at a wonderful price".
  6. Signal. Every stock gets one clear badge from a seven-step ladder (STRONG BUY · BUY · ADD · HOLD · WATCH · REVIEW · SELL/SWAP), plus exact price levels: "strong buy below $A, buy below $B". SELL fires only when the business itself breaks or a much better opportunity needs the money, which is how the Oracle sells. Each holding is tracked from BUY to SELL against the S&P 500.
  7. Context. The market-wide Buffett Indicator (total market cap ÷ GDP) appears as a "market temperature" gauge. You can also have it raise the margin-of-safety bar when the market is expensive.

2. Design principles

Explain everything. Every pass, fail and rank links to the numbers behind it, a 10-year sparkline and a plain-English reason. No black box.
Consistency over snapshots. The Oracle cared about 10 years of evidence, not one good quarter. Every rule is scored on its median and on how many years it passed.
Honest history. Backfills only use data that was public on each simulated date (point-in-time), so the backtest can't peek at the future.
Private and portable. No account and no cloud. Your data and settings stay in one SQLite file inside the folder.
Safe by default. Signals are tracked in a model portfolio, not your account. Placing real orders (a later phase) always needs your confirmation.
Pluggable data. Each data source sits behind an adapter, so you can swap free for paid, or add Schwab, without touching the screening logic.

3. Oracle criteria — the default "Oracle Classic" profile

The defaults combine your image (Brian Feroldi's income-statement rules of thumb, taken from Mary Buffett & David Clark's Warren Buffett and the Interpretation of Financial Statements) with the companion balance-sheet and cash-flow rules from that book. They also draw on Buffettology, Hagstrom's The Warren Buffett Way, Berkshire's published acquisition criteria and the shareholder letters.

Rule modes: Gate must pass or the stock is out · Scored adds to the Quality score · Buy gate must pass for a BUY signal (valuation) · Info shown but not scored. You can switch any rule between modes in a custom profile.

Lookback defaults to the last 10 fiscal years, with a minimum of 7. "Median" means the 10-year median. "Consistency" is the share of years that met the target.

H. Bank & insurer rulebooks

Why separate rules? A bank has no "gross margin" or "cost of goods". Its debt (customer deposits) is its raw material, so the standard rules would fail every bank on debt-to-equity and margins. That says nothing about whether it's a good bank. Insurers are similar: their "debt" is customer premiums held as float, which is the engine of Berkshire itself. The Oracle owns both kinds (American Express, Bank of America, Chubb), so Oracle Edge scores them with rules that fit:

What can't be automated. The Oracle's first filters are qualitative: is the business understandable (circle of competence), is management honest and owner-minded, and will the moat last 20 years? The app approximates these with numbers (rules 21–23, buybacks, margin stability). Each stock's report card also gets a short Your judgment checklist, and a stock you mark "outside my circle" is hidden from your rankings.

4. The Buffett Indicator ("market temperature")

What it is: total U.S. stock market value ÷ U.S. GDP. The Oracle called it probably the best single gauge of overall valuation, in a 2001 Fortune interview. It rates the whole market, not individual stocks, so the app uses it as a regime overlay rather than a per-stock filter.

≈ 260%

≈ 72–90%

How the app calculates it (free, daily):

  1. Numerator: the latest Federal Reserve Z.1 market value of corporate equities (from FRED, quarterly).
  2. Daily estimate: scale that by a total-market ETF's (VTI) price change since quarter-end, the same approach several public trackers use.
  3. Denominator: latest nominal GDP, annualized (FRED).
  4. Also show a detrended view (distance from the long-run trend in standard deviations), because the ratio has drifted upward for decades.

Added in v0.11: the Bitcoin early warning. A second market-temperature reading: Bitcoin's change over the last 20 trading days (a setting) against its own expanding history. A drop into its worst 20% so far has gone with a weak next month for QQQ; a rise into its best 20% with a strong one. Like the Buffett Indicator it never blocks a trade: it drives a recommendation on Today and a market alert in the daily email. Every day's call is logged and scored 20 trading days later, so the rule builds a record on data it has never seen.

5. Screening & ranking method

Most screeners give you a pass/fail list with no order. This one runs four stages so the top of the list is always the best combination of business quality and price.

Stage 3 — Quality score (0–100)

Each scored rule has three anchors: floor (0 pts), target (70 pts, the rule of thumb) and excellent (100 pts), with straight-line scoring in between. That gives partial credit, so a 39% gross margin isn't treated as a 5% one.

rule_score   = 0.6 × level_score(10-yr median) + 0.4 × consistency(% of years ≥ target)
                 − trend_penalty (if the metric is falling)

Quality      = Σ category_weight × avg(rule_scores in category)

  Profitability & moat (rules 1–8, 22–23) ......... 35%
  Returns on capital (19–21) ...................... 25%
  Balance-sheet strength (10–15) .................. 20%
  Cash & capital-lightness (16–18) ................ 10%
  Shareholder orientation (14, 21, dilution) ...... 10%

Example anchors for gross margin: floor 20% · target 40% · excellent 60%. For LT debt ÷ NI: excellent 0 yrs · target 4 yrs · floor 8 yrs.

Stage 4 — Value score: two conservative intrinsic-value models

Model A — Owner-earnings DCF
  • Start: owner earnings per share (5-yr normalized)
  • Growth g = lowest of: sustainable growth ROE×(1−payout), 10-yr EPS CAGR, 15% cap
  • 10 years at g, fading to 3% terminal growth
  • Discount rate = long-term (30-yr) Treasury yield, plus 2 pts while that yield is under 5%. This is the Oracle's own method: he discounts at the long-term government rate, adds a point or two when rates look low, and handles risk with a big margin of safety rather than a higher rate.
  • Result: IVA and implied return at today's price
Model B — Buffettology 10-year projection
  • Grow book value per share at ROE × (1 − payout) for 10 yrs
  • Year-10 EPS = avg ROE × projected BVPS
  • Year-10 price = EPS × 10-yr median P/E (capped at 20)
  • Add retained dividends
  • Expected return = (Year-10 value ÷ Price)1/10 − 1
Expected return (ER) = min(ER_A, ER_B)              ← always use the more conservative model
Intrinsic value (IV) = min(IV_A, IV_B)
Margin of safety     = 1 − Price ÷ IV
Value score (0–100)  = 0 at ER 5% · 70 at hurdle (10%) · 100 at ER ≥ 20%
The Oracle's return expectations, as built in. (1) Discounting: long-term Treasury rate, with no extra "risk premium". Risk is handled by demanding a big discount to value (the margin-of-safety gate). (2) Hurdle: The Oracle says he has never formally used a hurdle rate. In practice he has often passed on deals expected to return less than about 10%, and every candidate is compared with what bonds pay (rule 26). Default: 10%. (3) Stricter option: the Buffettology 15% profile uses the 15% bar from Mary Buffett's books. (4) Because a Treasury discount rate makes intrinsic values high for fast growers, growth is capped at sustainable growth, fades to 3% after 10 years, and only "quite certain" businesses (the ones that passed the gates) are valued at all.

Final Oracle Rank

Oracle Rank Score = Quality^0.65 × Value^0.35 × Confidence      (0–100)

Confidence  = 0.85–1.00 based on years of data, missing line items, restatements and
              whether net income and operating cash flow agree.
Tie-break   = higher margin of safety, then higher ROIC.
Why this formula? Multiplying (instead of averaging) means a weak score on either side drags the whole rank down, so a stock can't ride one great number. The 65/35 tilt toward quality encodes the Oracle's line that a wonderful company at a fair price beats a fair company at a wonderful price. Both exponents are profile settings, so you can test 50/50 or 75/25 in a backtest and let the rank-bucket report (§7) show which works better.

6. Buy / sell signals: the Oracle Edge ladder

Every stock always has exactly one badge, computed after each close for your portfolio. SWAP and the TRIM badges are the Oracle's three "sell part or all" cases: something better needs the money, the price got silly, or one position got too big. Each badge comes with a one-line reason and exact price levels, so the answer to "should I buy or sell this?" is never fuzzy.

Oracle-style selling, built in. The Oracle has given two reasons to sell: the business changed for the worse, or the money is needed for a clearly better opportunity. He also warns that delaying the fix for a mistake is the real sin. Selling after a price drop, or because a great company got expensive, is not on the list. (He has called his early McDonald's sale a big mistake.) The ladder follows exactly that.

Never a sell signal

  • A falling stock price on its own. If the business is intact, a lower price shows as ADD, not SELL.
  • A falling market, or a high Buffett Indicator. A hot market raises the bar for new buys but never forces a sale.
  • A single bad quarter. That's REVIEW, and only the next filing can confirm it.

Exact price levels for every stock (the "price ladder")

Each report card and list row shows where today's price sits against the stock's own levels. Example: intrinsic value $131 with a 25% margin-of-safety gate.

Levels move when the margin-of-safety gate moves (e.g. in today's "Extreme" market regime the BUY line drops to 40% below value). Every level is clickable and shows how it was calculated.

How signals reach you

  • Action list at the top of Today: "3 actions" with badge, reason and price levels, plus a Mark done button where you can record your actual fill price.
  • Email digest after each daily run, only when there's an action: subject like "Oracle Edge · ▲ BUY ABC · ▼ SELL XYZ". The body is a clean HTML card per action with badge, reason, price ladder and a link that opens the stock in the app. A weekly Sunday summary (portfolio vs S&P 500, new WATCH names, market temperature) is optional. Sent from your own email account. See "Email setup" in §13.
  • Windows notification at the same time with the one-line version: "▲ BUY ABC (Rank 84, buy below $98) · ▼ SELL XYZ (debt now 4.6 yrs of earnings)".
  • Every signal stores its reason as before → after values, so the Signals log reads like a journal: "SELL XYZ 2026-11-14: thesis broken — LT debt ÷ NI 3.6 → 4.6 (10-K), REVIEW since Aug 10-Q."
  • Signals are made after the close and meant to be acted on at the next open. The model portfolio records fills at the next open price.
  • Badges depend on what you own: see "Why there's a model portfolio" below.
Why there's a model portfolio (formerly "paper portfolio"). Three of the badges depend on whether you own a stock: HOLD vs WATCH, ADD, and every SELL/SWAP/TRIM. So the app has to know what's "owned". By default it assumes you followed every signal and keeps that record itself: the model portfolio. That gives you:
  • A complete buy/hold/sell answer for every stock from day one, with nothing to type in
  • The performance record in §7 (did following the signals beat the S&P 500?)
  • A way for SWAP and the oversized TRIM to work, since they need a portfolio with a size limit
It uses percentages only, so there's no dollar amount to set. The number of holdings is variable: every BUY is added and every SELL removed, so the portfolio grows or shrinks with the opportunities, the way the Oracle lets his cash pile up when nothing qualifies. Each buy starts at an equal weight (or 1.5× for STRONG BUY), then weights drift with prices. An optional holdings limit can be set if you want to force concentration, and the 40% size cap always applies. If you'd rather get badges for your real holdings, add them under My holdings (or sync from Schwab later). The app then shows badges for both, side by side.

6B. Price targets & alerts (moving averages, Fibonacci)

The rule: value decides what price is good; the chart only helps with when inside that range. Every buy and sell price comes from the valuation. Moving averages and Fibonacci levels never create a buy or a sale on their own. They only choose the limit price and how to split the buy.

What the research says

  • Fibonacci retracements (23.6/38.2/50/61.8%) are widely watched, but there is little solid evidence that they predict anything. The academic tests are small: one energy-sector sample (2017–2020) and a 4-stock, one-quarter test where only about 27% of support turns lined up with a Fibonacci level. Many traders watch these levels, which can make them self-fulfilling, so they are useful as places to rest a limit order, not as signals.
  • Moving-average timing (50/200-day): the longest study (155 years, Zakamulin) found no statistically significant edge in the recent half of the data. The rules give many false signals but tend to cut losses in bear markets. One study found the daily 200-day rule did worse than random timing on a risk-adjusted basis. Conclusion: no MA crossover sell signals; that would fight the Oracle's "sell only when the business breaks."
  • Value + momentum is the best-supported combination. Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (Journal of Finance, 2013) found value and momentum negatively correlated in every market, with the mix beating either alone. That justifies one modest timing tool: don't catch a falling knife. When a cheap stock is still sliding, buy in thirds rather than all at once.

Intrinsic valueValuation (Models A and B, lower used)Reference only. The Oracle doesn't sell at fair value.
Trim above2 × value (and P/E ≥ 2× its 10-yr median for 10 days)TRIM signal (unchanged)

SELL and REVIEW still fill at the next open whatever the chart says. A broken business isn't held for a bounce.

Alerts: "approaching" and "reached"

  • Watched: Buy Zone, pricey and near-miss stocks (suggested limit, buy-below, strong-buy); owned stocks (strong-buy add zone, trim level); plus your own alerts (any price, "falls to" or "rises to") added on the stock page.
  • Approaching = within 1.5 × the stock's average daily range (ATR-14), kept between 2% and 8%. A calm utility and a jumpy tech stock both warn about 1–2 typical days ahead.
  • Reached = the close is through the target, or the day's low/high touched it (a resting limit order would have filled).
  • No repeats: each target alerts once on approach and once on reaching. It re-arms only after the price moves back away by twice the band.
  • Delivery: after each close in the daily run, as a "Price targets" section in the email digest (or a short email of its own on days with no buy/sell signals), a Windows notification line and a "Price alerts" card on Today. The first scan after setup only records where everything stands, so you aren't flooded.
  • Intraday alerts need a real-time feed. That arrives with the Schwab integration (§17), using the same targets.

Proving it, not assuming it

A second locked profile, "Oracle Classic + timing", uses the same rules but delays each BUY while the price is below a falling 50-day average, for up to 60 signal days. Run both in Backtest from 2019 to see whether the timing filter actually adds return on your data before switching it on. It is off in Oracle Classic.

7. Performance tracking

Two views, because they answer two different questions:

A. Holdings: "did following the signals make money?"
  • One episode per BUY → SELL/SWAP (ADDs included)
  • Entry/exit price (next open), dividends, total return
  • S&P 500 (SPY) return over the same days → excess return
  • Max drawdown and max gain, days held, exit reason (thesis broken / swap)
  • Model portfolio equity curve vs SPY: CAGR, volatility, Sharpe, max drawdown, win rate
B. Buy-zone stints: "how did stocks do while they met the criteria?"
  • One stint per continuous stretch a stock spent in the Buy Zone, owned or not
  • Return from entering to leaving the zone, and for 1, 3 and 5 years after entry
  • Compared against SPY over the same window
  • Shows whether the criteria pick winners, separate from the sell rules
Is the ranking actually working? The rank-bucket report groups every BUY by its Rank at entry (#1–5, #6–15, #16+) and compares results. If top-ranked buys don't beat lower-ranked ones over time, the weights need tuning, and the report says so plainly.

How switching works

  1. Settings → Data Sources shows one row per data type with an ordered list of providers. Drag to reorder and paste a key if the provider needs one.
  2. Test pulls a sample (e.g. KO's last 5 closes) and shows speed, coverage and any errors before you save.
  3. Automatic fallback: if the first provider fails or returns gaps during the daily run, the next one fills in, and the run log says which source was used.
  4. Provenance: every price row and fact records its source, so mixed history is always traceable.
  5. Switch-over check: when you change the primary price source, the app compares the last 60 overlapping days between old and new and flags any ticker that differs by more than 0.5% (usually split or dividend adjustment differences).
  6. Re-sync history (optional): re-download all history from the new source, e.g. after upgrading to paid data with delisted tickers, then re-run backtests.
# every provider implements the same small interface — adding one never touches screening code
class PriceSource(Protocol):
    name: str
    needs_key: bool
    supports_bulk: bool          # whole market in one call (EODHD, Tiingo)
    supports_delisted: bool      # needed for honest backtests
    def daily_bars(self, tickers, start, end) -> DataFrame: ...
        # → date, ticker, open, high, low, close, adj_close, volume, dividend, split
    def health_check(self) -> Health: ...

class FundamentalsSource(Protocol):
    def facts(self, cik, since=None) -> DataFrame: ...   # concept, period, value, filed_date, accession
    def new_filings(self, since) -> list[Filing]: ...
What "free" costs you: free price sources can break without warning (yfinance especially) and mostly don't keep delisted stocks. Backtests run on free data are labeled "survivorship-biased: results likely optimistic". Upgrading later and pressing Re-sync history removes the label. Fundamentals are already complete and free from the SEC.
SEC rules: at most 10 requests per second, and a User-Agent with your name and email (asked in the setup wizard). Daily runs only re-pull companies that filed something new. Full refreshes use the bulk ZIP.

The hard part: XBRL normalization

Companies tag the same line item differently. Revenue alone may be Revenues, RevenueFromContractWithCustomerExcludingAssessedTax, SalesRevenueNet and others. A concept-map layer holds an ordered list of fallback tags per line item, derives missing fields (e.g. gross profit = revenue − COGS), and puts a data-quality flag on every computed value. The Data Doctor screen lists companies with missing or conflicting tags so the map improves over time. Plan for this to be about a third of the build.

9. Architecture & folder layout

Stack: one language, easy to share

  • Python 3.12, everywhere. Data, rules, scoring and the screens are all Python. Anyone who reads Python can maintain or extend it, with no JavaScript framework to learn.
  • NiceGUI (MIT license, actively maintained) for the interface. It turns Python into a polished browser app built on Vue/Quasar components, and has AG Grid tables and Apache ECharts charts built in. It runs on FastAPI, so the same server also offers a clean JSON API for later integrations.
  • SQLite (WAL mode) through SQLModel, with Alembic migrations so database upgrades are automatic when someone pulls a new version
  • uv for packaging: pyproject.toml + uv.lock pin exact versions, so it runs the same on every machine. uv.exe ships in the folder and installs a private Python into runtime\, with nothing installed system-wide.
  • Quality tooling: ruff (formatting/lint), pytest with "golden" tests (known companies' 10-K numbers must give known scores), and GitHub Actions to run them on every change
Sharing the code. The project is a GitHub repo with a README (screenshots, a 3-step quick start), MIT license, Start.bat for Windows and start.sh for Mac/Linux. Secrets, the database and the cache are excluded by .gitignore, so a friend clones it, double-clicks Start and gets the same setup wizard you did. Data-source plug-ins and rule definitions are plain files (rules/*.yaml, data_sources/*.py), so contributions don't require touching the engine.
Visual design. One consistent design system: a calm off-white/dark theme with a single brand green, one typeface (Inter), generous spacing and rounded cards. Badge colors are always paired with an icon and a word, so nothing relies on color alone. Numbers use tabular figures so columns line up, every chart shares the same palette, and the interface works in light and dark mode. Loading states show skeletons rather than spinners, and every empty state says what to do next.

Daily backup to the cloud folder. The app and its live database run from C:, which is fast and safe for SQLite. Once a day the database is backed up to the cloud-synced folder.
  • When: as the last step of each daily after-close run. You can also run it anytime with Settings → Back up now.
  • How: uses SQLite's built-in backup function, which takes a consistent copy even while the app is open. The copy is then integrity-checked, zipped (≈ 4–5× smaller), written to a temporary name in the backup folder and renamed when complete, so the sync tool never syncs a half-written file.
  • Retention: 14 daily, 8 weekly, 12 monthly. Older copies are pruned automatically. All three numbers are editable.
  • If the backup folder isn't available (sync drive not running): the backup retries every 15 minutes for 2 hours, then again at the next app start. The Today screen shows "Last backup: 2 days ago ⚠" until it succeeds.
  • Restore: Settings → Restore lists the backups by date. Picking one makes a safety copy of the current DB, then swaps the backup in. A fresh install on a new PC offers "Restore from latest backup?" during setup.
  • Also backed up (tiny): filter profiles and non-secret settings, inside the same zip. API keys are deliberately not included; re-enter them on a new PC.
  • Why not keep the live database in the synced folder? SQLite writes to its file constantly. If a sync tool rewrites the file while the app has it open, it can be corrupted, and pCloud users of other SQLite apps (Zotero, for example) have reported this. Backups are written once and never touched again, so syncing them is safe.

One-click behaviour (Start.bat): checks the runtime (installs it the first time) → starts the server → opens the browser → if the scheduled run was missed while the PC was off, offers to catch up. A tray-style status page shows whether a run is in progress.

Portability guard: the app records its own folder path. If you copy the folder somewhere else, it notices on the next start and offers to re-register the scheduled task at the new path.

11. Historical backfill / backtest

From the Backtest screen: pick a date range, a profile and a cadence (daily, or weekly for speed), then Run. The replayer steps through each trading day and runs the same engine as the live pipeline, with one difference: it can only see facts whose SEC filing date ≤ the simulated date, and prices up to that date.

  • Results go into their own run (a run_id), so backtests never mix with your live history. You can promote one to "seed history" if you want the live view to start from it.
  • Progress bar with ETA, pause/resume, and cancel. A finished run opens straight into the Performance view.
  • Settings: holdings limit (none by default), position cap, slippage/commission, fill rule (next open vs close).
  • Overfitting guard: when you tweak a profile and re-run, the app keeps the last ~2 years as a holdout and reports in-sample and out-of-sample results separately.
Decided: default backfill is 2019-01-01 → today with the strict 10-year rules. The setup wizard runs it once as part of the initial load, so the Performance view already has about 7 years of signals and episodes on day one.
One quirk of starting in 2019: SEC XBRL data starts in 2009 for the largest companies and covers all filers by about 2011. So in 2019–2020 the app can only measure a full 10 years for larger companies. Smaller companies phase in through about 2021. The backtest report shows the universe size by year so this is visible, not hidden. The 5-year "relaxed" mode and paid pre-2009 data stay available as options for later.
Survivorship bias: the SEC side already includes companies that later failed or were acquired. The price side needs a provider that keeps delisted tickers (Tiingo and EODHD do; free sources mostly don't). Without them, backtests look better than reality, and the app labels such runs accordingly.

20. Risks & known limits

  • Few or no buys right now. With the market near record valuations, strict Oracle rules may give an empty Buy Zone. That's the correct answer, not a bug, and the "pricey" list with buy-below prices is the useful output in that case.
  • XBRL messiness (custom tags, restatements, fiscal-year shifts, segment-only reporting). Covered by the concept map, quality flags and confidence factor.
  • The Oracle's real edge is qualitative. The app shortlists; you decide. The report card's "Your judgment" box exists for this reason.
  • Sector bias. These rules favor consumer brands, software, payments and asset-light businesses, and screen out capital-heavy ones by design.
  • Free price feeds break (yfinance especially). That's why sources are switchable with automatic fallback, and a paid source is a one-click upgrade.
  • Small caps and foreign filers have messier data (fewer tags, IFRS, currency). The confidence factor and Data Doctor make this visible rather than hiding it.
  • Not financial advice. The app is a research and signal-tracking tool.
